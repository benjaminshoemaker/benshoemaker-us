# Brief: The Product Builder Is Here

## Thesis

AI coding tools are collapsing the PM/engineer divide and creating a new hybrid role — the "Product Builder" — and companies are finally hiring for it. This isn't a prediction anymore. The job listings exist. The title is showing up on LinkedIn, at startups, and even at Meta and Walmart. Ben saw this coming, lived it at his startup, built a job search site for it, and now has the receipts.

## Prevailing Narrative

The mainstream take splits into two camps, both of which this post should push against:

1. **"AI will replace PMs/engineers"** — The fear-based take. WEF says 23% of jobs will transform by 2027. Hot takes about PMs being automated away. This misses the point — it's not replacement, it's convergence.

2. **"AI just makes PMs faster at PM work"** — The tepid take. "AI-augmented product management." PMs use AI for research, docs, and analysis, but engineering stays separate. MRJ Recruitment: "PMs in Big Tech are never going to touch the codebase with AI." This underestimates how much the boundary has already moved.

**The contrarian angle:** Neither replacement nor augmentation. It's a *new role* — one that didn't exist before because the cost of bridging PM and engineering was too high. AI tools collapsed that cost, and now companies are hiring for the result. The evidence is in the job postings themselves.

## Outline (Narrative Arc)

Open with concrete proof: actual job listings with titles like "Product Builder," "AI Solutions Builder," "Builder in Residence." These aren't engineering roles with a PM coat of paint — they explicitly require both product instincts and building capability. Hook: the role you've been predicting is now a job you can apply for.

Build to the "why now": AI coding tools (Cursor, Claude Code) made it possible for one person to hold both sides. The traditional PM/eng split existed because building was expensive and slow. When building gets cheap and fast, the split stops making sense for a large class of product work.

Ground it in personal experience: At Kinetic DI, the CEO/PM + Ben/engineer split was slower than Ben just running with ideas himself. This wasn't a skill issue — it was a structural one. The handoff was the bottleneck, not the people.

Address the skeptics: Big Tech still has separate PMs and engineers. Production code still needs real engineers. But the argument isn't that every company should merge these roles — it's that a new category of work has emerged where the merger is the right answer, and smart companies are hiring for it.

Close with the forward-looking bet: This role will be as common as "product engineer" or "growth engineer" within 2 years. The people who figure it out first — on both the hiring and career sides — will have a massive advantage.

## Personal Evidence (filled in)

### Kinetic BI Story
At Kinetic BI (PropTech startup), Ben was the technical co-founder; the CEO had deep domain expertise but wasn't technical. For the first month, they tried the traditional split: CEO writes requirements, Ben builds. It didn't work — specs were overly complex, had sprawl, and the CEO was using AI tools that dumped large volumes of text that wasn't actionable. Ben made a deliberate decision to just go build — "I've learned enough, I understand what we're trying to do, I'm just going to throw some stuff together and he can decide whether he likes it or not." Results: features took ~1 day instead of the multi-week handoff cycle, CEO liked ~80% of output, and they moved dramatically faster. Key insight: "Previously you would never do that because it was so costly to write all that code... a year ago as a PM I would have been like 'that's wasting my team's time.' Now it's like, it took me a day."

### Product Engineer vs. Product Builder Distinction
"Product engineer" has existed for years (Shopify, etc.), but required a high bar of technical contribution — you needed to be a strong engineer who also had product sense. The "Product Builder" is the same concept with a dramatically lower technical bar, enabled by AI coding tools. You need product intuition and enough technical understanding to direct AI agents effectively — not the ability to write production code from scratch. This opens the role to PMs, designers, and domain experts who couldn't have crossed the engineering bar before.
